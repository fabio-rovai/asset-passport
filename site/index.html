An Open Ontologies demo

Asset Passport

People are merging games with AI this autumn: Minecraft inside Elden Ring, Call of Duty with skateboarding. Nothing checks what a sword should become on the other side. Here are two small games of our own, a block-building game and a creature battler, with a border between them. Drag a thing from one game into the other. The border works out what it is, converts it under the receiving game's rules, and checks a proof before it gets in. Then it shows you what it does.

Smuggle a Capture Orb into Blockcraft

Blockcraft refuses capture devices: nothing in Blockcraft can own a mob. Several forgers tried to get Pocket Battlers' Capture Orb across anyway. The first few have no Pocket Battlers key. The rest are Pocket Battlers itself, signing with its real key. Each attempt below was run against the real engine when this page was built.

How the border works

Each game publishes a profile: its health scale and a mapping of its own words into one shared Game Asset ontology. A Blockcraft TNT is a ga:Explosive. A Pocket Battlers Capture Orb is a ga:CaptureDevice. With one hub, twenty games need twenty mappings instead of one hundred and ninety pairwise ones.

Work out what it is

The engine reasons over the thing with OWL rules and writes a certificate of every step. Blockcraft's profile says "anything made of diamond is Rock-typed", so the Diamond Sword's type is a conclusion, not a label someone typed. The receiving game runs its own copy of a Lean checker over the certificate.

Convert it

The receiving game declares how foreign numbers become its own. Here both declare the same policy: damage and healing keep their share of a player's health. 7 of Blockcraft's 20 HP is 35%, and 35% of a creature's 100 HP is 35. This is a policy, not a fact. The rule is published next to the games' profiles, so anyone can run it again on a passport.

Know who sent it

Each game signs the passports it issues, and publishes its public key in its profile. The receiving game checks the signature against its own copy of that profile, never against a key that arrives with the passport. A signature says who issued a passport and that nobody changed it since. It does not say the issuer told the truth, and the last card below shows exactly that.

Let it in, or not

The receiving game's border is a SHACL contract, run on Open Ontologies' SPARQL-compiling evaluator and on its verified evaluator, and a thing crosses only when both agree. Losses are warnings: it enters, and its passport names what had nowhere to go.
